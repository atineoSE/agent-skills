# Examples

A running list of concrete before/after fixes pulled from real runs of this
skill: a flat line and the rewrite that fixed it, a jargon sentence and its
plain version, and so on. Each entry is small — one fix, one lesson.

**Cap: twelve entries.** When a thirteenth would be added, first look for an
existing entry whose lesson a newer entry already teaches and drop that one;
if no entry is redundant, drop the oldest entry instead. Keep the list short
enough to skim in full.

Add an entry here whenever the user edits a handed-over script by hand — the
edit is a correction, and the diff between what the skill wrote and what the
user changed it to is the lesson. Format each entry as:

```
### <short label>
- Before: "<the flat/jargon/broken line, quoted>"
- After: "<the fixed line, quoted>"
- Lesson: <one line on what this teaches, and which RENT element it serves>
```

Seed entries below, drawn from `reference/worked-example.md`:

### Citation parentheticals don't speak
- Before: "(Shoda, Mischel & Peake 1990)"
- After: "a nineteen-ninety study by Shoda, Mischel, and Peake"
- Lesson: a citation parenthetical reads as noise aloud; turn it into a
  spoken attribution or cut it. Serves Ease.

### Numerals as digits sound wrong read aloud
- Before: "552 children"
- After: "five hundred fifty-two kids"
- Lesson: spell out numbers as words in the script text so the narrator
  reads them naturally, not as a string of digits. Serves Ease.

### Findings-first beats background-first
- Before: opening on "Walter Mischel's Stanford experiments... found that
  preschoolers who could delay eating a marshmallow..." (the source
  document's own order: background, then finding)
- After: "You've probably heard about the marshmallow test... Here's what
  usually gets left out: ... most of that story fell apart."
- Lesson: open on what the audience already half-knows, then the reversal —
  not on the study's own chronology. Serves Relatability and Novelty.

### The antithesis pair is the loudest AI tell
- Before: "The principle stands. The quotation doesn't."
- After: "So the tradition really does ask its leaders to think about the
  unborn. The sentence everyone quotes was written much later."
- Lesson: "X is true, Y isn't" reads as machine-written, and a script that
  leans on it makes every paragraph sound the same. Say both facts plainly.
  Serves Ease, and the `simple-English` AI-tells rule.

### Fragment lists sound like advertising read aloud
- Before: "Not privatized. Not nationalized. Self-governed."
- After: "Nobody had privatized them and no government had taken them over.
  The users ran them."
- Lesson: stacked fragments look punchy on the page and sound like a
  commercial in a narrator's voice. Write the sentence out. Serves Ease.


Entries below are harvested from the first full run of the skill — a
twenty-minute piece built from a research map on short-term gratification.
All three are structural rather than line-level, and all three come from the
same underlying mistake: the script toured its source instead of answering one
question.

### A list-shaped payoff, narrated instead of taught
- Before: "What is more useful is that the successful cases share features. It
  is clear who is in and who is out. The people who bear the costs are the
  ones who write the rules. Somebody is actually monitoring, and that somebody
  answers to the users rather than being parachuted in from outside. Penalties
  start small and escalate, so that one mistake does not end your membership.
  There is a cheap and fast way to settle disputes. And the outside
  authorities leave them alone enough to organize. None of this requires
  anyone to be virtuous."
- After: three of those conditions, one beat each, with a case attached — for
  example: "Start with the simplest one: everybody knows who is in. In the
  Swiss alpine villages Ostrom studied, grazing rights on the high pasture
  belong to households in the village, and the list is not vague. That sounds
  trivial until you try to run a fishery where anyone with a boat counts as a
  member. You cannot ask people to hold back for a group whose edges nobody
  can name." Then the same treatment for who writes the rules, and for
  penalties that escalate — and one sentence saying the researchers found
  several more conditions, without listing them.
- Lesson: eight conditions in ninety seconds is the whole payoff delivered as
  a run of assertions; a listener half-hears all of them and keeps none. Cut
  to three, give each an example the source can support, and say the rest
  exist. Serves `The one question` — give the payoff room.

### An ending that opens a loop instead of closing one
- Before: "So here is the question I would rather ask than whether you have
  willpower. What has your world taught you about waiting, and did it earn
  that lesson?"
- After: "So here is the answer, as far as the evidence goes. Groups hold
  together when the structure makes cooperating the sensible choice — clear
  membership, rules written by the people who pay for them, consequences that
  start small — and not when the people in them are unusually good. That is
  the finding, and it is contested at the edges. What it asks of you is a
  different question: what has your world taught you about waiting, and did it
  earn that lesson?"
- Lesson: the reflective closing question is good writing, but it cannot be
  the last thing said — it leaves the listener holding a question instead of
  an answer. State the answer in one repeatable sentence, then reflect.
  Serves `The one question` — the ending answers it.

### A hook that opens a different question than the ending answers
- Before: opening on personal willpower — "Here's something you already know
  about yourself... in the actual moment of choosing, you know that you are
  not choosing it" — in a piece whose ending is about how institutions are
  designed.
- After: keep the personal on-ramp, then turn it toward the actual question
  inside the first minute: "...you know. You do it anyway. Now hold that, and
  make it bigger. Because the interesting question is not why you break a
  promise to yourself. It is why whole villages, sharing one fishery for four
  hundred years, somehow don't — and what they built that the rest of us
  haven't."
- Lesson: the warm personal opening is worth keeping, but it has to arrive at
  the question the piece will actually answer. An on-ramp is fine; advertising
  a different question means the loop never closes. Serves `The one question` —
  the hook opens what the ending answers.

Entries below come from the user listening to a finished episode and naming
what went wrong by ear. Both are loop failures, and both looked fine on the
page.

### A loop opened in a subordinate clause
- Before: "Which means anyone with a genuinely good car gets offered less than
  it is worth, and takes it off the market. Which means the average quality of
  what is left drops."
- After: "Now think about what that does to a person who genuinely has a good
  car. They get offered the price of an average car, and they know their car
  is better than average. So they do not sell it." Then, after a beat: "That
  car does not vanish. The owner keeps driving it and waits. Or they sell it
  quietly to a cousin, or to somebody at work, in the one corner of the market
  where the buyer already has a reason to believe them."
- Lesson: "takes it off the market" reads as one clause and lands as three
  questions — why would they, where does the car go, what happens to it. A
  mechanism the listener cannot picture is an open loop. Show the person
  deciding, and say where the thing ends up. Serves `Loops`.

### Trailing off instead of declining out loud
- Before: "Researchers name others. How much risk each side can carry. Whether
  you are the one who makes the first offer. Whether there is anyone else at
  the table at all. But those three carry most of it."
- After: "Researchers have found others, and I am going to leave them alone,
  because those three carry most of the weight on their own."
- Lesson: naming three more factors and explaining none opens three loops in
  four seconds. Saying plainly that more exist and that you are not covering
  them closes the loop; trailing off leaves it open. Serves `Loops`.
