# Worked example

One paragraph from a research map on short-term gratification versus
long-term thinking, turned into a spoken hook and the one section that
follows it. Audience for this run: adults who've heard of "the marshmallow
test" casually — as a parenting or self-help idea — but don't know its
research history.

**The one question this piece answers:** if the marshmallow test doesn't
predict how a child turns out, what was it actually measuring? Every choice
below is downstream of that question — the hook opens it, the section builds
toward it, and the closing sentence answers it. Settling that question with
the user is step 2 of the procedure, and it happens before any of this gets
written.

This is a hook plus one section plus the closing sentence, not a full script.
It exists to show the *kind* of transformation the skill is after, not to be
copied.

## Before (source, verbatim)

> **The marshmallow test and its replication crisis.** Walter Mischel's
> Stanford experiments (Shoda, Mischel & Peake 1990) found that preschoolers
> who could delay eating a marshmallow to get a second one later showed
> strong correlations with later academic and socioemotional success. This
> became one of psychology's most famous findings. However, a widely cited
> conceptual replication by Tyler Watts, Greg Duncan, and Haonan Quan
> (*Psychological Science*, 2018), whose key analysis focused on a subgroup
> of 552 children whose mothers had not completed college, found the
> predictive power substantially diminished. In their words, the bivariate
> correlation "was only half the size of those reported in the original
> studies and was reduced by two thirds in the presence of controls for
> family background, early cognitive ability, and the home environment. Most
> of the variation in adolescent achievement came from being able to wait at
> least 20 s." Their conclusion: interventions that only teach delay, without
> changing broader cognitive and environmental capacities, would likely have
> very small effects; an additional minute of waiting at age 4 predicted only
> about 1/10th of a standard deviation gain in age-15 achievement.
> *Counter-critiques:* Some researchers argued Watts et al.'s correlations
> were biased toward zero by censoring of waiting times, and that many of the
> "controls" were themselves part of the delay-of-gratification construct.
> Related work (Kidd et al. 2013) showed that delay behavior reflects
> rational beliefs about the reliability of the environment/reward, not just
> innate self-control.

## After (spoken script)

You've probably heard about the marshmallow test. A kid sits alone with a
marshmallow. An adult tells them: wait a few minutes without eating it, and
you'll get a second one. Wait, the story goes, and you're set up for life —
better grades, better relationships, more self-control, years down the
road. That version of the story made it into parenting books and
commencement speeches for decades. Here's what usually gets left out: when
other researchers ran a bigger, more careful version of the same test, most
of that story fell apart.

In twenty eighteen, a team led by Tyler Watts, Greg Duncan, and Haonan Quan
tried to replicate the original nineteen-ninety study, with a much larger
and more carefully chosen group of children. Their sharpest results came
from five hundred fifty-two kids whose mothers hadn't finished college — a
group the original study barely touched. The link between waiting for a
marshmallow and doing well later on was still there, but it was weak. Once
you accounted for a child's family background, their early thinking skills,
and their home life, that link shrank to a third of its original size.
Waiting one extra minute at age four predicted only about a tenth of a
standard deviation in how that same kid did at fifteen. That's a small
number. Practically, it means just teaching a four-year-old to wait longer,
without changing anything else about their life, probably won't move the
needle much on its own. Not everyone agreed with that reading — some
researchers pointed out that cutting off the longest wait times in the data
can bias a correlation toward zero, and that several of the things Watts and
his colleagues controlled for were arguably part of self-control itself, not
separate from it. And a separate study adds a wrinkle worth sitting with:
kids who don't wait might not be short on willpower at all. They might just
have good reason not to trust that the second marshmallow is ever coming.

## Closing sentence (how the piece ends)

So the best current answer is that the marshmallow test was measuring a child's
read on their own circumstances more than their character — how much a promise
from an adult was worth where they lived. Researchers still argue about how
much of the original effect survives, and that argument is not settled. But it
does move the question. It stops being what is wrong with this child, and
starts being what has this child learned to expect.

## What each move is doing

- **Opening with "you've probably heard"** puts the audience's own prior
  knowledge on the page in the first sentence — Relatability. It also sets
  up the reversal that follows, and lands on the piece's question — what was
  the test actually measuring — inside the first minute, which is the same
  question the closing sentence answers.
- **"Here's what usually gets left out... most of that story fell apart"**
  states the twist before any methodology — Novelty and Tension together:
  it names the gap between the popular belief and the evidence up front,
  rather than building up to it.
- **Numbers spelled as words** ("five hundred fifty-two," "a tenth of a
  standard deviation") and the citation parenthetical dropped in favor of
  "a team led by Tyler Watts, Greg Duncan, and Haonan Quan" — Ease, and the
  script constraint on numerals and citations.
- **"That's a small number. Practically, it means..."** translates a
  statistic into a plain consequence before moving on — Ease, keeping the
  order need-to-know.
- **Ending the *section* on the counter-critiques and the trust
  explanation** — Tension: a mid-piece section reopens the gap rather than
  settling it, so there is a reason to keep listening. This is section-level
  behaviour, and the opposite of how the piece as a whole ends.
- **The closing sentence answers the question in one plain statement a
  listener could repeat** — "measuring a child's read on their own
  circumstances more than their character" — and only then widens out. Note
  that it names the live disagreement instead of pretending the matter is
  closed, and that it says what the evidence supports rather than what anyone
  should do. Ending instead on the reflective line, with no answer before it,
  is the failure `The one question` exists to prevent.
